Case study · Stock-market research

A backtesting system built to stop me fooling myself

A research setup for Indian equities where every result is priced the way real money would be, and every idea is attacked before it is believed.

01The task

Find out whether any stock-selection strategy really beats the market after costs and taxes, and whether my own trading habits help or hurt.

02Before

  • Results came from a shortcut calculation that quietly assumed free, tax-free daily rebalancing.
  • That shortcut inflated returns three times once and almost five times again.
  • New ideas kept re-appearing in a different form, and the research went in circles.

03Who does what

The AI does

  • Pulls and cleans free exchange data: prices, corporate actions, delivery volumes.
  • Runs each study through one "honest engine" with real share counts, real cash, trading costs and taxes.
  • Runs a fixed health check before any result is trusted.
  • Writes a plain-words verdict and a findings entry after each study.

I do

  • Form the hypotheses, in business terms.
  • Decide what counts as a fair test before seeing the result.
  • Read every verdict and decide what gets saved.
  • Keep a single findings file: open questions come before new ideas.

04Result

  • Every earlier result was re-priced on the honest engine, and the inflated ones were withdrawn, not just caveated.
  • One strategy survived 10 rounds of attacks (luck, survivorship, liquidity, execution) and a walk-forward test, and still beat the market out of sample.
  • From 457 closed trades: 63% had peaked well above the eventual sell price. A simple take-profit rule would have beaten my actual exits, and every stop-loss rule did worse.

What broke, and what I learned

My first, most exciting numbers were wrong, and they came from the calculation method, not the strategy. Fixing it meant throwing away results I liked. Now there is one engine that prices money, a reference check that must pass, and a rule that nothing is believed until it survives an attack.
